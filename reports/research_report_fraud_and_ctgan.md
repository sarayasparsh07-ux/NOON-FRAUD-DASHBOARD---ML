# Seed honestly, then make CTGAN prove itself

Build the seed dataset as a **mixture of five fraud typologies at about 3% overall prevalence** (defensible range 2–5% for a "fraud plus policy abuse" label, versus 0.1–0.5% for confirmed card fraud alone), anchored on the few public UAE figures: an average restaurant-delivery ticket of **about AED 77**, **3.8–6.7 orders per customer per month**, and a payment mix led by cards and Apple Pay with cash on delivery (COD) in single digits to low teens. No UAE or GCC platform, Noon included, publishes fraud data, so every fraud-conditional magnitude is an assumption, but the directions are well supported, and the most important one is that **account takeover (ATO) strikes old accounts on new devices while promo abuse and stolen-card fraud strike brand-new accounts**, which makes fraud's account-age distribution bimodal. A genuine CTGAN means **ctgan 0.12.1**, which is source-available under the **Business Source License 1.1, not MIT**, fitted only on training-split fraud rows, with batch_size cut from 500 to about 100, categorical and binary columns declared discrete, and every output rounded and clipped because standalone ctgan does neither. Its loss curves prove training happened but do not measure quality; that job belongs to SDMetrics 0.32.0's unified QualityReport, LogisticDetection, and a classifier two-sample test, each benchmarked against a naive per-column bootstrap. Published evidence predicts that CTGAN will at best match SMOTE and class weighting at roughly 800 fraud rows, so the comparison must use average precision against the prevalence baseline, a validation-chosen threshold for every arm, sigmoid recalibration, paired bootstrap intervals and repeated seeds. Because the analyst writes the data-generating rules, every result demonstrates the method and none measures real-world fraud detection.

## Five fraud typologies leave five different fingerprints

Industry sources agree that food delivery suffers several overlapping fraud and abuse types but disagree on their weights, largely because each vendor sees a different slice. Ravelin's January 2025 survey of 1,466 fraud professionals ranked **card-not-present (CNP) fraud (~53%) and fraudulent chargebacks (~50%)** as the costliest types for online marketplaces, ahead of ATO (~29%), promo abuse (~20%) and refund abuse (~17%), though the survey had no MENA respondents and its labels were read off a chart ([Ravelin 2025 survey](https://pages.ravelin.com/hubfs/FS25%20Fraud%20and%20Payments%20Survey%202025/Online%20Marketplace%20Fraud%20Trends%20by%20Ravelin.pdf)). Incognia, which scores behaviour rather than payments, found **refund abuse and promo abuse each made up 48%** of consumer-side fraud it detected on food-delivery and ride-hailing apps ([Incognia](https://www.incognia.com/newsroom/gig-economy-fraud-report-shows-refund-abuse-representing-48-percent-of-consumer-fraud-in-2024)), while Sift's chargeback-driven benchmark attributes **99.6% of food-and-delivery fraud incidents to credit cards** ([Sift](https://sift.com/blog/food-delivery-fraud-benchmarking-risk-and-tracking-trends/)). The lesson for the challenge is that is_fraud must be defined explicitly, because its prevalence and its typology mix both follow from that definition.

Account takeover is unusually common in this vertical. Sift reports an ATO attack rate of about **20% for food ordering and delivery against 2.5% across all industries** ([Deseret News](https://www.deseret.com/business/2024/05/16/hacked-food-delivery-apps/)), but Sift defines that rate as blocked, pending or failed logins over total logins ([Sift FIBR](https://sift.com/blog/new-2024-fraud-industry-benchmarking-resource-data/)), so it is a login-level figure and must not be fed into the generator as an account compromise rate. Ravelin's analysis of takeovers at one food-delivery client supplies the behavioural shape: **71% were monetised through orders, 46% ordered to a different city or region, 48% changed the phone number, and attackers placed 3–4 orders on average** ([Ravelin](https://www.ravelin.com/blog/what-do-fraudsters-do-after-they-take-over-an-account)). Ravelin's signal sequence runs from a contact-detail change to a new-device login within 24 hours to an order at a new address, and it investigates only when two or more signals coincide ([Ravelin](https://www.ravelin.com/blog/six-ways-to-spot-account-takeover)). Translated into the challenge's columns, ATO is a high account_age_days combined with device_change = 1, location_mismatch = 1 and a burst in num_orders_last_7d.

Promo abuse looks almost the opposite. In a Ravelin referral case, **about 5% of referral vouchers** were flagged as abusive, roughly 90% of the abuse came from a few hundred users, and accounts were linked through shared email, phone, device ID and payment method ([Ravelin](https://www.ravelin.com/blog/case-study-delivery-promo-abuse)); in DataVisor's sign-up-bonus ring at an Asian food platform, **every order was under $5** and hundreds of accounts sat on one device ([DataVisor](https://www.datavisor.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/TopFoodDelivery-Final.pdf)). Promo abuse is therefore a very new account, a discount, a near-minimum basket and almost no history, and its real tell is many accounts per device, which a per-account device_change flag cannot see. Refund ("item not received") abuse is first-party fraud that surfaces after delivery and is detected mainly through refund frequency and linked-account history ([Ravelin](https://ravelin.com/solutions/refund-abuse)). Stolen-card fraud carries the classic CNP flags of high-value orders from new accounts, international cards with domestic delivery, and velocity spikes ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/resources/more/payment-fraud-red-flags)); older Stripe network data showed fraud peaking late at night and **non-US fraudulent transactions running about twice the normal value** ([Digital Transactions on Stripe](https://www.digitaltransactions.net/quiet-online-shopping-days-yield-the-biggest-fraud-spikes-stripe-says/)), though that 2016 evidence is neither recent nor food-specific.

The table converts this evidence into generator settings. The typology shares are **assumptions**, loosely anchored on Incognia's 48/48 refund–promo split and on Ravelin's ranking of CNP and ATO as the costliest types; no source gives the typology mix for any UAE platform.

| Typology (assumed share of fraud) | account_age_days | order_amount | payment_method | Other signals | Evidence anchor |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Promo / multi-account (30–35%) | Very new (<7 days) | Near-minimum basket | Card, wallet | discount_applied ≈ 1; num_orders_last_7d 0–1; device_change low | DataVisor, Ravelin referral case |
| Refund / INR abuse (25–30%) | Any, often established | Mid to high | Any, including wallet and COD | prev_refunds 2–6+ | Ravelin refund frequency, Incognia |
| Stolen card / CNP (20–25%) | New | ~1.5–2.5× legitimate median | Card | location_mismatch elevated; night uplift; prev_refunds 0 | Stripe red flags and 2016 data, Sift 99.6% |
| Account takeover (10–15%) | Old (often >180 days) | Above the user's norm | Stored card, wallet | device_change ~0.6–0.9; location_mismatch ~0.45; 3–4 order burst | Ravelin ATO analysis |
| COD fake orders (3–5%) | New | High | COD | No refund history | Qualitative only ([Quiqup](https://www.quiqup.com/post/how-to-reduce-rto-in-uae)) |

Generating each fraud row by first sampling a typology and then sampling features conditional on it produces the non-monotonic patterns real fraud shows, such as the bimodal account age, which a single logistic link from features to fraud cannot. Keep the typology as a hidden column that never enters the classifier or CTGAN; it becomes the most valuable diagnostic later. The ten columns also lack the strongest signals vendors rely on, namely accounts per device, accounts per card or phone, and linked-account graphs ([Ravelin](https://www.ravelin.com/blog/case-study-delivery-promo-abuse); [Incognia](https://www.incognia.com/newsroom/incognia-mobile-app-fraud-insights-report-reveals-food-delivery-apps-are-major-target-for-location-based-fraud)), so promo abuse will be the hardest typology to catch, and the write-up should state that rather than plant an artificially strong promo signal.

## Legitimate UAE orders centre near AED 77 and one to two orders a week

The best recent UAE anchor is Syrve restaurant point-of-sale data showing an **average delivery ticket of AED 77.35 in 2025 and AED 76.97 in H1 2026** ([Khaleej Times](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/uae/uae-food-delivery-orders-rise-15-in-h1-as-diners-shift-away-from-eating-out)), consistent with 2018 averages of AED 73 on Talabat and AED 75 on Zomato ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/entertainment/arts-culture/revealed-why-uae-is-hooked-on-takeaway-meals-1.1978216)). The tail is long and legitimate: Careem's 2025 records include a **single food order above AED 1,600** and one user who placed 41 orders in a day ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/business/retail/from-47-million-burgers-to-dh4600-grocery-baskets-what-people-ordered-online-in-the-uae-in-2025-1.500386456)). A lognormal with a median of AED 55–65, a mean of AED 75–85, a floor near AED 15 and a clip at AED 2,000 fits these points, although only the mean is evidenced. For frequency, talabat discloses **6.7 orders a month on average, 3.8 for food-only customers and 13.0 for multi-vertical customers** ([talabat Investor Presentation](https://ir.talabat.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/20250224_talabat_Investor-Presentation.pdf)), and its highest-value customers exceed 30 a month ([Zawya](https://www.zawya.com/en/press-release/companies-news/talabat-reports-strong-results-for-q3-2025-and-reiterates-guidance-for-the-full-year-jb08x02p)). That supports a negative-binomial num_orders_last_7d with a legitimate mean of about 1.2–1.6 and a real tail above 20, which means high velocity is a weak fraud signal in this market.

Payment data is e-commerce-wide and internally inconsistent. Worldpay figures presented by Antom put the 2025 UAE online mix at wallets 35% (Apple Pay dominant), credit cards 27%, debit cards 15% and **COD about 7%**, with shares that exceed 100% once BNPL is added ([Antom](https://www.antom.com/markets/uae)), while an earlier Worldpay edition gave credit cards 37% ([Arabian Business](https://www.arabianbusiness.com/resources/worldpay-empowers-uae-merchants-with-easier-payment-solutions-amid-growing-e-commerce-demand)). Checkout.com reports MENA COD preference **halving from 41% in 2020 to 20% in 2023** ([Checkout.com](https://www.checkout.com/newsroom/checkout-coms-4th-annual-mena-report-finds-cash-on-delivery-usage-halved-amongst-maturing-digital-economy)), and talabat said around 2023 that more than 70% of its payments were online ([AGBI](https://www.agbi.com/articles/cashless-economy-is-at-the-door-says-uae-food-delivery-boss/)). Since food delivery has historically used more cash than e-commerce overall, a legitimate mix of roughly **card 50%, applepay 30%, wallet 5–10% and cod 8–15%** is a reasonable inference, with a COD sensitivity range of 5–25%. Giving applepay lower stolen-card risk than raw card entry rests on tokenisation and biometrics, and no public source quantifies that gap in the UAE.

Distance and time of day rest on the weakest anchors. The only measured restaurant-to-customer distance comes from London in 2017, with a **mean and median of 2.2 km** ([UCL](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10120813/3/Wise_Cities%20paper%20-%20Meal%20deliveries%20-%20September%202020%20revised%20version.pdf)); UAE sprawl justifies assuming a longer median of 3–4 km with a 12–15 km cap, and distance should carry little or no fraud signal. The same London study found **about 63% of meals collected between 17:00 and 22:00**, while Careem data shows a normal UAE evening grocery peak at 19:00–20:00 and a **70% jump in 4 am grocery orders during Ramadan** ([Khaleej Times](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/uae/uae-4am-grocery-orders-up-by-70)). The legitimate hour_of_day should therefore be bimodal with a meaningful late-night tail, which dilutes the classic finding that fraud concentrates around 3 am ([Information Security Buzz on Sift](https://informationsecuritybuzz.com/seven-habits-highly-fraudulent-users/)).

Every risky signal has a common legitimate explanation in the UAE, and the generator should reflect that. Dubai received **18.72 million international overnight visitors in 2024** ([Romania Insider on DET data](https://www.romania-insider.com/news/dubai-international-visitors-2024)), so tourists and expatriates holding home-country cards make location_mismatch common among legitimate orders. Families and colleagues share phones, so device signals need confirming factors ([Ravelin](https://www.ravelin.com/blog/six-ways-to-spot-account-takeover)). False positives are costly too: **23% of MENA consumers reported a false decline in the previous three months**, and a third would switch provider after one failed payment ([Checkout.com](https://www.checkout.com/newsroom/checkout-coms-4th-annual-mena-report-finds-cash-on-delivery-usage-halved-amongst-maturing-digital-economy)). The resulting seed specification follows; every numeric parameter is an assumption unless the evidence column names a source.

| Feature | Legitimate distribution (assumed) | Fraud direction | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| order_amount | Lognormal, median 55–65, mean 75–85, clip ~2,000 | Bimodal: CNP/ATO high, promo near minimum | Mean moderate (Syrve); fraud multiplier weak (Stripe 2016) |
| account_age_days | Right-skewed; ~5–10% of orders under 30 days | Bimodal: new (promo, CNP) and old (ATO) | Direction qualitative; shares assumed |
| hour_of_day | Lunch and dinner peaks, ~8–12% late-night tail | Flatter, ~1.5–3× uplift at 00:00–05:00 | Old (Stripe 2016, Sift 2014) |
| num_orders_last_7d | Negative binomial, mean 1.2–1.6 | ATO/CNP bursts; promo 0–1 | Legitimate strong (talabat); fraud moderate |
| payment_method | card ~50, applepay ~30, wallet 5–10, cod 8–15 | Card carries CNP; wallet promo/ATO; COD fake orders | Mix moderate; fraud by method weak |
| delivery_distance_km | Gamma, median 3–4, cap 12–15 | Slight or no shift | Weak (London 2017 only) |
| discount_applied | Bernoulli 0.30–0.45 | Promo ≈ 1; overall fraud ~0.6–0.7 | Typology share from Incognia; legitimate rate assumed |
| prev_refunds | Zero-inflated, mean 0.1–0.3, scales with tenure | Refund abusers 2–6+ | Direction qualitative only |
| device_change | Bernoulli 0.02–0.05 | ATO ~0.6–0.9; CNP moderate | Direction from Ravelin; rates assumed |
| location_mismatch | Bernoulli 0.10–0.20 | ATO ~0.45; CNP elevated | ATO share from Ravelin; baseline assumed |

Prevalence spans more than an order of magnitude depending on what the label counts. Confirmed card fraud sits at **0.172% in the ULB dataset and 0.13% in PaySim** ([arXiv 2512.13040](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.13040)), and Sift's food-and-delivery chargeback rate is just **0.048%**. Blocked or attempted fraud runs far higher: Sift's food-and-delivery block rate was **3.1% in Q2 2025** ([Sift](https://sift.com/blog/food-delivery-fraud-benchmarking-risk-and-tracking-trends/)), IEEE-CIS records 3.5% ([arXiv 2512.13040](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.13040)), and Feedzai's Bank Account Fraud suite, itself generated with CTGAN, shows **0.85–1.5%** ([Jesus et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.13358)). A 3% default for fraud plus abuse therefore mirrors both Sift's block rate and IEEE-CIS. Two figures should never serve as base rates: DataVisor's "20 fraud cases per 100 orders" at one client is an undefined vendor claim ([DataVisor](https://www.datavisor.com/blog/case-study-how-one-company-used-datavisor-to-combat-food-delivery-fraud-during-covid-19)), and the **40% of shoppers** who admitted some fraud or abuse in a year ([Ravelin](https://www.ravelin.com/blog/friendly-fraud-trends-survey-report)) counts people, not orders. Because the test set needs at least 100 and ideally 200 frauds, a 60/20/20 split at 3% prevalence calls for a seed of **about 45,000 rows**, which yields roughly 810 training frauds and 270 test frauds. Before any modelling, confirm that every planted relationship has the intended sign, that no single feature reaches an AUC near 1, and that fraud's account age is visibly bimodal.

## ctgan 0.12.1 runs under BUSL and needs small batches and clipping

The latest stable releases are **ctgan 0.12.1 (13 February 2026)** and **sdv 1.38.5 (28 September 2026)**, both supporting Python 3.9–3.14, with ctgan requiring **torch ≥ 2.6.0 on Python 3.13** ([PyPI ctgan](https://pypi.org/project/ctgan/); [PyPI sdv](https://pypi.org/project/sdv/)). Any description of ctgan as MIT-licensed is out of date: the library moved to the **Business Source License 1.1 at version 0.7.0 in January 2023**, and 0.6.0 was the last MIT release ([PyPI ctgan 0.7.0](https://pypi.org/project/ctgan/0.7.0/)). The licence bars offering a commercial "Synthetic Data Creation Service" to third parties and converts each release to MIT four years after it ships ([CTGAN LICENSE](https://github.com/sdv-dev/CTGAN/blob/main/LICENSE)), so coursework and internal analytics fall inside the grant, but the tool should be described as source-available rather than open source. **SDMetrics 0.32.0 remains MIT** and installs without torch ([PyPI sdmetrics](https://pypi.org/project/sdmetrics/)). With an SDV 2.0.0.dev0 pre-release already out and sdv 1.38.5 pinning rdt below 2.0 ([SDV HISTORY](https://github.com/sdv-dev/SDV/blob/main/HISTORY.md)), pinning `ctgan==0.12.1`, `sdmetrics==0.32.0` and `rdt<2` avoids API churn.

The 0.12.1 constructor defaults are `embedding_dim=128, generator_dim=(256,256), discriminator_dim=(256,256), generator_lr=2e-4, discriminator_lr=2e-4, batch_size=500, discriminator_steps=1, log_frequency=True, epochs=300, pac=10, enable_gpu=True`, and the working methods are `fit(train_data, discrete_columns=())`, `sample(n, condition_column=None, condition_value=None)`, `set_random_state(seed)`, `save(path)` and `CTGAN.load(path)` ([ctgan.py](https://github.com/sdv-dev/CTGAN/blob/main/ctgan/synthesizers/ctgan.py)). Passing `epochs` to `fit` now raises a DeprecationWarning, the `cuda` argument is deprecated in favour of `enable_gpu` ([_utils.py](https://github.com/sdv-dev/CTGAN/blob/main/ctgan/synthesizers/_utils.py)), and `load` calls `torch.load(weights_only=False)`, which unpickles arbitrary code, so only self-produced model files should ever be loaded ([base.py](https://github.com/sdv-dev/CTGAN/blob/main/ctgan/synthesizers/base.py)). Mechanically, CTGAN (Xu et al., NeurIPS 2019) encodes each continuous column with a **Bayesian Gaussian mixture of up to 10 modes**, trains a conditional generator by sampling discrete categories in proportion to their log frequency, and optimises a WGAN-GP objective through a **PacGAN critic that sees 10 rows at a time** to resist mode collapse ([arXiv 1907.00503](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.00503); [data_transformer.py](https://github.com/sdv-dev/CTGAN/blob/main/ctgan/data_transformer.py)). The mode-specific normalisation suits a bimodal fraud account age well.

The defaults were not designed for about 800 fraud rows, and four pitfalls follow directly from the source code. First, steps per epoch equal `max(len(train_data) // batch_size, 1)`, so **800 rows at the default batch of 500 yield only 300 generator updates in 300 epochs**, while a batch of 100 yields eight steps per epoch; batch_size must also be even and divisible by `pac`, and a violation fails during `fit` with a bare AssertionError ([ctgan.py](https://github.com/sdv-dev/CTGAN/blob/main/ctgan/synthesizers/ctgan.py); [SDV docs](https://docs.sdv.dev/sdv/single-table-data/modeling/synthesizers/ctgansynthesizer)). Second, standalone ctgan performs **no min/max clipping**, its reverse transform `normalized × 4 × std + mean` can produce negative distances and order values, and integer columns are **cast by truncation rather than rounded** ([data_transformer.py](https://github.com/sdv-dev/CTGAN/blob/main/ctgan/data_transformer.py); [rdt numerical.py](https://github.com/sdv-dev/RDT/blob/main/rdt/transformers/numerical.py)); SDV's `CTGANSynthesizer` clips and rounds by default, which is the main argument for using it ([sdv ctgan.py](https://github.com/sdv-dev/SDV/blob/main/sdv/single_table/ctgan.py)). Third, the `sample` docstring says a condition only "will increase the probability" of a value, so fitting a fraud-only model is far more reliable than conditioning a two-class model on is_fraud, and SDV's reject-sampling `sample_from_conditions` is the route to exact conditions ([sdv base.py](https://github.com/sdv-dev/SDV/blob/main/sdv/single_table/base.py)). Fourth, deep generators "yield significantly lower-quality minority examples than majority examples" ([D'souza et al., AAAI 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.15657v1)), and a typology holding 10–15% of 800 training frauds amounts to only 80–120 rows, so ATO and COD fakes are where coverage will fail first.

| Column | CTGAN treatment | Post-processing |
|---|---|---|
| order_amount, delivery_distance_km | Continuous, float | Clip to domain floor and cap; round to 2 dp |
| account_age_days | Continuous, cast to float | Round, clip at 0 |
| hour_of_day | Discrete (24 valid levels, blocks impossible values) | None |
| num_orders_last_7d, prev_refunds | Discrete if few observed levels, otherwise continuous | If continuous: round, then clip at 0 |
| payment_method, discount_applied, device_change, location_mismatch | Discrete (string or int) | Compare marginals against the real rows |
| is_fraud | Dropped before the fraud-only fit (constant) | Re-attached as 1 |

```python
from ctgan import CTGAN
fraud_tr = train_df[train_df.is_fraud == 1].drop(columns=['is_fraud'])   # training split only
fraud_tr[['order_amount','delivery_distance_km','account_age_days']] = \
    fraud_tr[['order_amount','delivery_distance_km','account_age_days']].astype(float)
DISCRETE = ['payment_method','discount_applied','device_change','location_mismatch','hour_of_day']
m = CTGAN(epochs=1000, batch_size=100, pac=10, verbose=True, enable_gpu=False)  # even, divisible by pac
m.set_random_state(42)
m.fit(fraud_tr, discrete_columns=DISCRETE)
m.loss_values.to_csv('ctgan_loss.csv', index=False)   # training evidence
m.save('ctgan_fraud.pkl')
syn = m.sample(n_needed)                                # then round, clip, record % out of range
```

The artifacts that prove the GAN is genuine are the per-epoch loss CSV, the saved model, the seed, the pinned versions and **the share of out-of-range rows before clipping**, which is a fidelity finding in its own right.

## Losses show that training happened; SDMetrics and detection tests show quality

CTGAN's logged losses come straight from its WGAN-GP code. The discriminator loss is `−(mean(D(real)) − mean(D(fake)))`, the negative of the critic's Wasserstein estimate with the gradient penalty excluded; the generator loss is `−mean(D(fake))` plus a conditional cross-entropy term; and **each epoch records only its last mini-batch**, not an epoch average ([ctgan.py](https://github.com/sdv-dev/CTGAN/blob/main/ctgan/synthesizers/ctgan.py)). DataCebo describes the healthy pattern as a generator loss that drifts negative and stabilises while the discriminator loss stays near zero, notes that "lower loss values – even if they are negative – mean that the neural networks are doing well", and names losses that never stabilise as the warning sign ([DataCebo](https://datacebo.com/blog/interpreting-ctgan-progress/)). SDV adds that more epochs help "up until a certain inflection point" ([SDV docs](https://docs.sdv.dev/sdv/single-table-data/modeling/synthesizers/ctgansynthesizer)). In practice, plot a rolling mean over the noisy last-batch values, keep training while the generator loss is still falling at the final epoch, treat sustained oscillation or divergence as failure, and never quote a final loss as a quality score, since the generator level depends on the critic's arbitrary offset and no official threshold exists.

Quality assessment belongs to SDMetrics, whose API changed in July 2026. Since **version 0.28.1, the recommended classes are the unified `sdmetrics.reports.QualityReport` and `DiagnosticReport`**, which take dicts of `{table_name: DataFrame}` plus metadata with a `'tables'` key, while the old `single_table` report classes still work but emit a FutureWarning ([SDMetrics HISTORY](https://github.com/sdv-dev/SDMetrics/blob/main/HISTORY.md); [reports/\_\_init\_\_.py](https://github.com/sdv-dev/SDMetrics/blob/main/sdmetrics/reports/__init__.py)). Column Shapes scores each marginal with KSComplement (1 − KS D) or TVComplement (1 − total variation distance), and Column Pair Trends uses correlation and contingency similarity ([column_shapes.py](https://github.com/sdv-dev/SDMetrics/blob/main/sdmetrics/reports/single_table/_properties/column_shapes.py); [column_pair_trends.py](https://github.com/sdv-dev/SDMetrics/blob/main/sdmetrics/reports/single_table/_properties/column_pair_trends.py)). The trap is that the unified report **averages only pairs whose real correlation exceeds 0.5 or whose real association exceeds 0.3** ([quality_report.py](https://github.com/sdv-dev/SDMetrics/blob/main/sdmetrics/reports/quality_report.py)); fraud features are usually weakly correlated, so Pair Trends can return NaN and the overall score silently collapses to Column Shapes alone. Both thresholds should be set to 0 before calling `generate`, and the report should say so. DiagnosticReport's Data Validity property penalises out-of-range values ([diagnostic_report.py](https://github.com/sdv-dev/SDMetrics/blob/main/sdmetrics/reports/diagnostic_report.py)), and LogisticDetection fits a 3-fold logistic regression to separate real from synthetic rows and returns `1 − mean(2·max(AUC, 0.5) − 1)`, so **1.0 means indistinguishable and 0.5 corresponds to a fold AUC of 0.75** ([detection/base.py](https://github.com/sdv-dev/SDMetrics/blob/main/sdmetrics/single_table/detection/base.py)). DataCebo calls Column Shapes and Pair Trends above 80% "a great score" and recommends tracking them across epoch checkpoints such as 300, 600 and 1,000 ([DataCebo](https://datacebo.com/blog/interpreting-ctgan-progress/)).

```python
from sdmetrics.reports import QualityReport, DiagnosticReport
from sdmetrics.single_table import LogisticDetection
meta = {'tables': {'fraud': {'columns': {
    'order_amount': {'sdtype': 'numerical'}, 'account_age_days': {'sdtype': 'numerical'},
    'payment_method': {'sdtype': 'categorical'}, 'device_change': {'sdtype': 'boolean'}, ...}}}}
qr = QualityReport(); qr.real_correlation_threshold = 0; qr.real_association_threshold = 0
qr.generate({'fraud': real_fraud_tr}, {'fraud': syn}, meta, verbose=False)
qr.get_properties(); qr.get_details('Column Shapes')
DiagnosticReport().generate({'fraud': real_fraud_tr}, {'fraud': syn}, meta, verbose=False)
LogisticDetection.compute(real_fraud_tr, syn, meta)
```

Fidelity scores mean little without a baseline. Score a **per-column independent bootstrap**, which preserves every marginal while destroying all joint structure, and SMOTE-NC output against the same real rows; if CTGAN does not beat the bootstrap on Pair Trends and Detection, it has not learned the joint structure, and the ATO cluster of old account, device change and location mismatch is exactly that kind of structure. High fidelity also does not guarantee usefulness, since a 2026 study found fidelity and downstream classifier performance **weakly negatively correlated (ρ = −0.30)** ([Won et al., Electronics 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/15/4/883)). Checking typology coverage directly, for example the share of synthetic frauds that are old accounts with device_change = 1 against the training frauds, catches collapsed modes that a single global score hides.

A classifier two-sample test goes deeper than LogisticDetection. Lopez-Paz and Oquab's procedure labels rows by source, trains any classifier and scores it on held-out rows, with held-out accuracy distributed approximately as **N(½, 1/(4·n_te))** under the null ([arXiv 1610.06545](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.06545v4)). For 800 real against 800 synthetic rows with pooled out-of-fold predictions, the null standard deviation of AUC is about 0.014, and a permutation test with 200–1,000 label shuffles gives a valid null for cross-validated AUC. The design decisions matter more than the statistic. CTGAN emits floats with many decimals while the seed is rounded, so a gradient-boosted tree will separate the sets on precision alone, the tabular equivalent of the checkerboard artifacts that made image-GAN tests trivially separable in the original paper. Remove such artifacts first, de-duplicate and balance the two sets, run both a logistic regression (which catches marginal shifts) and a gradient-boosted tree (which catches interaction differences), explain any separation with permutation importance and per-feature AUC, and treat an AUC well below 0.5 as a bug. Near-perfect indistinguishability paired with near-copies of real rows is a privacy red flag rather than a success ([SDMetrics docs](https://docs.sdv.dev/sdmetrics/data-metrics/metrics-in-beta/detection-single-table)), so add a distance-to-closest-record check. For marginals, report effect sizes such as KS D, Wasserstein distance in the column's own units ([SciPy](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.wasserstein_distance.html)) and total variation distance rather than p-values, because the 5% KS critical value at 800 versus 800 rows is about 0.068 and any difference becomes "significant" once tens of thousands of synthetic rows are compared.

## Published evidence predicts parity, so design the comparison to detect it

The literature gives no licence to assume that GAN augmentation wins, and its pattern is consistent: GANs sometimes beat SMOTE, mostly in the authors' own enhanced variants, while simple controls remain competitive.

| Study | Setting | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| [Fiore et al. 2019](https://ricerca.uniparthenope.it/handle/11367/64699) | GAN trained on fraud rows | Improved sensitivity; the abstract does not report specificity |
| [Engelmann & Lessmann](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.09202) | Conditional WGAN-GP, credit scoring | Beat SMOTE variants on most datasets, but "no oversampling method performs generally better than its alternatives" and "not altering the class distribution often delivers competitive results" |
| [Elor & Averbuch-Elor 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08528) | Strong vs weak classifiers | Balancing "does not improve prediction performance for the strong ones" |
| [Zhao & Guan 2023](https://peerj.com/articles/cs-1634/) | Plain CTGAN vs SMOTE on three credit datasets | CTGAN never beat SMOTE on G-mean and lost on every metric on the Taiwan data; generated frauds sometimes landed in the legitimate region |
| [Won et al. 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/15/4/883) | 3,712 minority rows, about 4× this project's | **SMOTE gave the best recall and F1**; default CTGAN and TVAE "underperformed compared to simpler methods" |
| [D'souza et al., AAAI 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.15657v1) | Modern generators at 1.5–2% minority | Generators often beat SMOTE, but CTGAN's results were "more mixed" |

With about 800 training frauds and a gradient-boosted classifier, the honest prior is that **CTGAN at best matches class weighting**, and any comparison that omits class weighting is incomplete, as most fraud-GAN papers are. Leakage is the other recurring flaw, because resampling before the train–test split "may have displayed artificially inflated performance metrics" ([Kabane 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07437)). The structural fix is to split first and fit every sampler, CTGAN included, only on training-fold frauds, wrapping CTGAN as a custom `fit_resample` sampler inside an imbalanced-learn `Pipeline` so it is refit inside every cross-validation fold ([imbalanced-learn pitfalls](https://imbalanced-learn.org/stable/common_pitfalls.html)). The arms should be no rebalancing, `class_weight` or `scale_pos_weight`, random oversampling, SMOTENC from imbalanced-learn 0.14.2 (compatible with scikit-learn 1.9, and since 0.14.2 raising an error when the categorical encoder does not keep one column per category, per the [release notes](https://imbalanced-learn.org/stable/whats_new.html)), and CTGAN at one or two synthetic ratios, all sharing one tuned learner and one tuning budget. Running a weaker learner alongside gradient boosting is worthwhile, since balancing helps weak learners most.

Average precision is the headline metric, always printed next to prevalence. ROC curves do not change with the class ratio, while **the precision–recall curve does, and its no-skill baseline equals prevalence** ([Saito & Rehmsmeier 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0118432)). Use `average_precision_score`, a non-interpolated step sum, rather than trapezoidal PR-AUC, which scikit-learn warns "can be too optimistic" ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.metrics.average_precision_score.html)). At 3% prevalence an AP of 0.30 is a tenfold lift, and every arm must be scored on the same natural-prevalence test set, because AP values from different prevalences cannot be compared.

Thresholds must be chosen per arm, by one pre-declared rule, on data the model never trained on. scikit-learn calls the default 0.5 cutoff "most certainly not ideal" and warns against tuning thresholds on training data ([scikit-learn threshold guide](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/classification_threshold.html)). SMOTE, GAN augmentation and class weights all inflate predicted fraud probabilities: up-weighting fraud by a factor k makes a 0.5 cutoff behave roughly like **1/(1 + k)** on the unmodified model, and rebalancing 3% fraud to 50:50 implies k ≈ 32, so "SMOTE at 0.5 versus baseline at 0.5" mostly measures a threshold shift. In simulation, van den Goorbergh and colleagues found that resampling did not improve AUROC and that **simply shifting the threshold achieved similar sensitivity and specificity without distorting probabilities** ([van den Goorbergh et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.09101v1)), echoing Elkan's advice to train on the data as given and make cost-optimal decisions from the probabilities ([Elkan 2001](https://mlanthology.org/ijcai/2001/elkan2001ijcai-foundations)). `TunedThresholdClassifierCV` works if its default `scoring='balanced_accuracy'` is overridden; a "maximum recall at precision ≥ p₀" or alert-budget rule needs a short custom function over `precision_recall_curve`, since no built-in constraint parameter exists. Pooled out-of-fold predictions give more stable thresholds than one small validation split, and `FixedThresholdClassifier(FrozenEstimator(model), threshold=t)` freezes the result so the test set is scored exactly once.

Calibration needs the current API and a method that suits SMOTE and GANs. `CalibratedClassifierCV(cv="prefit")` was deprecated in scikit-learn 1.6 and **no longer appears in the 1.8 or 1.9 documentation** ([1.6 changelog](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/whats_new/v1.6.html); [1.9.1 docs](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.calibration.CalibratedClassifierCV.html)); the replacement is `CalibratedClassifierCV(FrozenEstimator(fitted_pipeline), method="sigmoid")` fitted on a disjoint, un-resampled calibration split, or the whole imbalanced-learn pipeline passed with `cv=StratifiedKFold(5)` so each calibration fold is scored at natural prevalence. Sigmoid is the right default, since scikit-learn advises against isotonic below about 1,000 calibration samples and favours sigmoid "for very uncalibrated classifiers on very imbalanced datasets". The analytic prior-shift correction p = r·p_s / (r·p_s + 1 − p_s), with r = 1/w, suffices for class weights but not for SMOTE or GANs, which reshape the fraud feature distribution rather than just the prior; a 2026 preprint found analytic correction failed to repair SMOTE miscalibration while Platt or isotonic recalibration largely did ([Liu 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29720)), though the paper is unreviewed and internally inconsistent about the size of the fix. Report the Brier score with its skill score against the constant predictor's π(1 − π), which is **0.0291 at 3% prevalence**, plus log loss and a reliability diagram with quantile bins, because uniform bins push almost every prediction into the first bin.

Uncertainty must cover both the test sample and training randomness. Collins and colleagues recommend **at least 100, ideally 200, events** to validate a prediction model ([Collins et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4738418)), clinical guidance applied here by analogy, and even at 200 test frauds a recall near 0.8 carries a 95% interval of about ±0.055. The primary comparison is a **paired, stratified bootstrap of the metric difference**, resampling the same indices for both models and reporting the interval of ΔAP; `scipy.stats.bootstrap(paired=True)` shares indices but cannot stratify, so a short loop that resamples frauds and legitimate rows separately is needed ([SciPy](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.bootstrap.html)). McNemar's test on frozen decisions, in its exact binomial form when discordant pairs number fewer than about 25–50 ([Raschka 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.12808)), works best run separately on frauds and legitimate orders so the majority class does not dominate, DeLong's test covers ROC-AUC ([DeLong et al. 1988](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/712661)), and a Holm correction handles the four arm-versus-baseline comparisons. Because the bootstrap ignores training randomness and GANs are unstable, the whole pipeline of split, augmentation, fit, calibration and threshold should be repeated over 5–10 seeds, a judgement call rather than a sourced standard. Claim an improvement only when its sign holds across seeds and its paired interval excludes zero, and include the ablation of the baseline at a tuned threshold so ranking gains are separated from threshold shifts.

### Self-generated data demonstrates the method, not real fraud detection

Train-on-synthetic, test-on-real (TSTR) is defined as training on generated data and testing "on a held-out set of true examples" ([Esteban, Hyland & Rätsch 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02633)), and its evidential value comes entirely from that real test set. Nothing in this challenge is real: the analyst writes the seed rules, CTGAN learns them, and the classifier is tested on more data drawn from them. The circularity therefore operates at three levels, by reasoned argument rather than any peer-reviewed analysis of fraud benchmarks. A high AP on the seed holdout shows only that the classifier recovered the signals the analyst planted, at the strength and noise level the analyst chose. A CTGAN gain shows at most that CTGAN reproduced the simulator well enough to add useful fraud rows. SDMetrics scores and two-sample tests between CTGAN output and the seed measure **fidelity to the seed, not realism**, since neither dataset is ground truth, and even hitting a target overall AUC band of 0.80–0.92 confirms the design rather than the world.

Framed correctly, this is a legitimate controlled benchmark with the advantage of known ground truth and adjustable prevalence, signal strength and label noise. The defensible framing labels every result as produced "on simulated data generated under stated assumptions" and publishes the assumption tables above. It adds sensitivity runs over prevalence (2–5%), COD share (5–25%), typology mix and label noise to test whether the method ranking holds, plus a shift test that trains on one simulator version and evaluates on another with altered fraud patterns as the closest available stand-in for adaptive fraudsters. The hidden typology column should drive **recall by typology**, which will show where each method helps and will most likely confirm that promo abuse stays hard to catch under the ten-column schema whatever the augmentation. Where time allows, rerunning the identical protocol on IEEE-CIS at 3.5% fraud ([arXiv 2512.13040](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.13040)) offers an external check on whether the CTGAN, SMOTE and class-weighting ranking survives contact with real fraud, even though that dataset is not food delivery.

## Conclusion

The strongest possible outcome here is a defensible null result. "CTGAN matched class weighting within its paired confidence interval, at many times the compute cost" is consistent with the published evidence and shows that the leakage, threshold and calibration controls worked. A large CTGAN gain on this setup should instead trigger an audit for a GAN fitted on leaked validation or test rows, a 0.5 threshold silently favouring the resampled arms, a test set scored at the wrong prevalence, or fraud planted so cleanly that any extra copies help.

The typology-mixture seed turns the circularity into an asset, because knowing which typology produced each fraud row lets the project show *where* augmentation helps rather than merely *whether* it does. Rare, structurally distinct typologies such as ATO, with roughly 100 training rows, are exactly where a GAN either adds coverage or collapses, while missing device-graph features cap promo-abuse detection whatever the method. The natural next step with real data is to add accounts-per-device and refund-rate features, since the leakage-safe pipelines, per-arm thresholds, sigmoid calibration and paired bootstrap built here carry over unchanged.
